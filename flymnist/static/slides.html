  • Everything on this chart is measured in one unit: teaspoons of sugar. One teaspoon is about 4 g of sugar, 16 kcal, or 67 kJ of energy.
  • Your brain uses about 20 watts, less than a light bulb. So one teaspoon of sugar keeps it running for about an hour (67 kJ ÷ 20 W ≈ 56 minutes). A whole day of thinking: about 26 teaspoons. (The 20 W is about 20% of a resting body's ~100 W.)
  • The scale jumps by 10× at each step, otherwise the small bars would be invisible. The GPT-3 bar is about 69 million times the "1 hour" bar.
  • One chatbot question is cheap. Google says a typical Gemini text question uses 0.24 Wh (2025, counting the whole data centre; Google's own report, not peer-reviewed). That's about 1/80 of a teaspoon, so one teaspoon ≈ 80 questions. Epoch AI estimates about 0.3 Wh for a ChatGPT question; long "thinking" answers use more.
  • Training is the expensive part. Training GPT-3 once took about 1,287 MWh (Patterson et al., 2021). Your brain over an 80-year life uses about 14 MWh. So training GPT-3 ≈ 92 human lifetimes ≈ 7,300 years of one brain. Newer models are much bigger: Epoch AI estimates Grok 4's training at about 310 GWh, roughly 1.8 million brain-years.
  • The fly: its brain uses about 256 nanowatts (Panda et al., Cell Reports Methods 2026, measured on brains taken out of the fly, so a living one may differ). Over its ~2-month life that's about 1.3 joules, 1/50,000 of a teaspoon. Your brain uses about 80 million times more power, but it also has about 600,000 times more neurons.
  • Fair warning: it's not a perfect comparison. Training a model is not the same as living a life, and GPT-3 knows facts no single person does. But the gap is still huge.
  • Question for the room: why is the brain so cheap? A clue from our fly model: while it looks at one digit for 0.1 seconds, only about 11,500 of its 138,639 neurons fire even once, and in any tiny moment fewer than 1 in 1,000 are firing. Neurons only spend energy when they fire. A computer chip, in contrast, does every multiplication at every step, even when nothing changes. We come back to this on the "how the model works" slide.

Google & Janelia: the whole nervous system of a male fly

Brain plus the nerve cord that runs down the body to the legs (the fly's "spinal cord"): 166,000+ neurons. A map only, not yet a simulation.

research.google ↗
  • Three pieces work together today: a map, a simulation that runs on the map, and a body.
  • FlyWire = the map. Scientists took the brain of one adult female fruit fly and cut it into 7,062 slices, each only about 40 nanometres thick, over 1,000 times thinner than a hair (the FAFB dataset, Zheng et al., Cell 2018). An electron microscope photographed every slice (about 21 million images). Then AI (from Princeton) plus hundreds of volunteers and scientists traced every neuron through all the slices and found every connection (Dorkenwald et al. and Schlegel et al., Nature 2024). Result: 139,255 neurons, about 54.5 million synapses (connection points), more than 8,400 cell types. Our files are version 783: 138,639 neurons and 15.1 million neuron-to-neuron links. Think of it as a road map: it tells you which streets exist and which connect, but not where the cars are going.
  • Shiu et al. = the simulation. They gave every neuron simple rules ("add up incoming signals, fire when you reach a threshold") and let signals flow along the FlyWire map. Like simulating traffic on the road map. Only one number was tuned by hand: how strong one connection point is. It predicted which neurons make a fly start eating sugar or clean its antennae, and 91% of 164 predictions were then confirmed in real flies (Nature 2024). They used an older map version (v630); the open-source code we build on (github: eonsystemspbc/fly-brain) uses v783.
  • NeuroMechFly = the body. A virtual fly with legs, joints and wings in a physics engine (EPFL, Nature Methods 2022 and 2024). Brain signals can move it, like a video-game character. We use it on the gesture slide.
  • The newest map (Google Research + HHMI Janelia + Cambridge, Cell, September 2026): the whole nervous system of a male fly: brain, the parts behind the eyes and the nerve cord that runs to the legs. More than 166,000 neurons and 125 million synapses. Google's AI did the tracing. It's only a map so far, but it will let future models connect the brain all the way to the legs. (Fun fact: Google did not trace FlyWire; Princeton did.)
  • What we add today: showing pictures to the fly's eyes, reading what its neurons do, and running everything right in your phone's browser.

A neuron is an electrical gate

Say these out loud (they used to be on the slide):

  • Inputs charge it up. Signals coming in raise the neuron's voltage; on its own the voltage slowly leaks back down.
  • Threshold → spike. If the voltage crosses a line (the threshold), the neuron fires a spike: all or nothing, and always the same size.
  • Stronger input = more spikes per second, not bigger spikes.
  • So the message is in when and how often a neuron fires.

Background:

  • Picture a leaky bucket: input pours water in, a small hole lets it leak out. When the water reaches the rim (the threshold), the neuron fires a spike and the bucket is emptied (reset).
  • A real spike is about 100 millivolts tall and lasts 1–2 milliseconds, every single time. So the size of a spike tells you nothing; only the timing and the count do.
  • Brighter light or a harder touch → more spikes per second. After each spike the neuron needs a short rest (the refractory period), which sets a speed limit on how fast it can fire.
  • In the demo: turn the input up and watch the firing get faster; a weak input never reaches the threshold at all.

How the brain model works

  • Each neuron adds up its inputs, slowly leaks, and fires when it reaches a threshold. Then it rests a moment.
  • Each connection: strength from the wiring map, excite (+) or inhibit (−), arrives after a tiny delay.
  • Only neurons that fire do any work. That's why it runs on a phone.
  • We just saw one neuron. Now: how do neurons talk to each other? Next slide we'll wire many of them into a full circuit, from the eyes to the muscles.
  • This is exactly the model from Shiu et al. 2024, with the same rules and numbers. Scientists call it a "leaky integrate-and-fire" model: integrate = add up the inputs, leaky = the charge slowly drains away, fire = spike at the threshold. It's the leaky bucket from the previous slide.
  • In the demo: fire the exciting neuron and watch the receiving neuron's voltage jump up a little after a tiny delay; fire the inhibiting one and the voltage is pushed down. Fire the exciting one several times quickly and the receiver reaches the threshold and fires.
  • The numbers, if anyone asks: a resting neuron sits at −52 millivolts and drains back there in about 20 milliseconds. At −45 millivolts it fires, drops back to −52 and must rest for 2.2 milliseconds. Each incoming signal fades away over about 5 milliseconds.
  • Connection strength = how many connection points (synapses) the two neurons share × plus or minus (exciting or inhibiting, from the chemical the sender uses) × 0.275 millivolts. That 0.275 is the only number the scientists tuned. A signal takes 1.8 milliseconds to arrive.
  • Input: we pick some neurons (for example the eye cells) and make them fire randomly at a chosen speed. Output: we can listen to any neuron we like.
  • The computer moves forward in steps of 0.1 milliseconds. Remember the energy slide: at any moment only a tiny fraction of neurons fire. So in each step we only process the connections of the neurons that just fired and skip everything else. That's why 0.1 seconds of fly brain takes only about 0.4 seconds in a laptop browser, and it matches the scientists' original code spike for spike.

Neurons wire up into circuits

  • Sense → decide → move.
  • A connection can excite (push the next neuron to fire) or inhibit (hold it back).
  • Something rushes at the fly → a "looming detector" fires → the "giant fiber", the brain's fastest wire to the legs → the fly jumps away.
  • Two mirror-image halves, left and right, joined by a crossing link. Brake neurons hold the signal back.
  • On the previous slide we saw how two neurons talk. Now the whole cycle: every nervous system does the same basic loop, sense → decide → move. Sensor neurons feel or see something, "interneurons" in the middle process it, and motor neurons tell the muscles what to do.
  • The demo shows both halves of the brain, mirror images of each other: each eye feeds its own detectors and its own giant fiber, plus a weaker crossing link to the other side. "Brake" neurons are inhibitory: when they fire, they push the giant fiber's voltage down. Try switching the brakes off, or cutting the left–right link, and watch how the firing changes.
  • Connections come in two kinds. Exciting ones push the next neuron toward firing; inhibiting ones hold it back. Which kind it is depends on the chemical messenger (neurotransmitter) the sending neuron uses. In the fly model, acetylcholine excites; GABA and glutamate inhibit.
  • The escape jump (why it's so hard to swat a fly): when something grows fast in the fly's view, like a hand or a fly swatter, special "looming detector" neurons fire. Their scientific names are LPLC2 and LC4. They drive the "giant fiber" (DNp01), a very thick, very fast neuron running from the brain toward the legs. It triggers the jump within a few thousandths of a second.
  • We tested this in our simulation. Switch on the looming detectors and the giant fiber fires 170–190 times per second, while the walking and turning neurons stay quiet.
  • Show it to the right eye only: the left giant fiber fires 100 times per second, the right one 190. Cut every link between the left and right half of the brain and the left fiber drops to 0. So the left fiber was getting its signal from the other side of the brain.
  • Nobody programmed this in. It comes straight out of the wiring map.

The Drosophila brain: who does what

Over half the brain is for seeing.

  • Optic lobe seeing (lamina, medulla, lobula, lobula plate)
  • Visual projection neurons carry detected features (motion, looming) to the central brain
  • Mushroom body learning and memory
  • Central complex inner compass, navigation
  • Lateral horn inborn reactions to smells
  • Antennal lobe first stop for smells from the antennae
  • SEZ taste and feeding
  • Descending neurons wires from the brain to the body
  • This is the real map. Every dot is one neuron, sitting exactly where it is in the FlyWire brain. Drag to spin it, scroll to zoom. The whole brain is smaller than a poppy seed.
  • More than half of all the neurons are for seeing: flies live by their eyes. The neurons in the two big side parts (the optic lobes) process the image in layers. Their scientific names are lamina → medulla → lobula and lobula plate; the lobula plate is all about motion.
  • Visual projection neurons are feature detectors: each kind looks for one thing, like an object coming closer (the looming detectors from the circuit slide), and sends the result to the centre of the brain.
  • The centre of the brain: the mushroom body learns and remembers (a later slide shows how); the central complex is the inner compass for finding the way; the lateral horn holds inborn reactions to smells ("rotten = go away"); the antennal lobe is the first stop for smell; the taste centre (its scientific name is SEZ, the subesophageal zone) decides whether to eat.
  • Descending neurons: only about 1,300 cells carry every command from the brain down to the body. It's a bottleneck, like a narrow neck, and it's exactly what we read on the gesture slide.
  • This slide uses the scientific names. Other slides use plain names for the same parts: Eye cells = eye input (photoreceptors and lamina); Seeing = optic lobe; Vision messengers = visual projection neurons; Centre of the brain = central brain (mushroom body, central complex, lateral horn, antennal lobe, SEZ); Wires to the body = descending neurons.
  • Counts in our model: eye input (the cells we stimulate) 18,266; optic lobe 69,846; visual projection 8,038; central brain 32,379; descending 1,299; other 8,811. Total 138,639.

Using the fly brain as a neural network

The fly brain is never trained. Only a tiny "reader" on top learns.

  • Like guessing which stone was thrown into a pond from the ripples: the pond never learns, only the guesser does.
  • A digit is shown to the fly's eyes: brighter pixel → that eye cell fires faster (up to 500 times a second), for 0.1 s.
  • We count how often each neuron fired. The reader (a simple pattern-matcher) learns which counts mean which digit.
image→eyes→brain: never changed→firing counts→reader: trained→digit
  • The big idea is called reservoir computing. You take something big and complicated that you don't change (here: the fly brain) and let it turn the input into a rich pattern of activity. Then a very simple model learns to read that pattern. Pond analogy: throw a stone in, look at the ripples, guess the stone. The pond never learns anything; only the guesser does.
  • The "reader" is a logistic regression: the simplest kind of trainable classifier. It gives each neuron's count a weight and adds them up, one score per digit; the highest score wins. We train one reader per brain region, so we can ask which region "knows" the digit.
  • How the picture gets into the fly: the eye cells (in the lamina, the first layer behind the eye) lie on a curved sheet. We flatten that sheet and lay the 28×28-pixel digit on top. Each eye cell fires randomly, faster for brighter pixels, up to 500 times a second, for 100 milliseconds (0.1 s). Both eyes see the same digit.
  • 500 times a second is faster than real eye cells. We need a strong input because the model has no background activity and the first eye layer is only partly mapped, so a weak signal would die out before it reaches the deep parts of the brain.
  • Fair-play rule: the eye cells we stimulate directly are not given to the readers. So a reader can only recognise the digit if the information really travelled through the brain's connections.

How a fly learns what to like

  • Smell + sugar at the same time → the fly learns to like that smell.
  • Smell + shock → it learns to avoid it.
  • Both use dopamine, sent by different neurons.
  • Start with a question: "Have you ever felt sick after some food and then couldn't stand its smell?" Flies do the same thing, and we know exactly which neurons do it.
  • Where it happens: a part of the fly brain called the mushroom body (it really looks a bit like a mushroom). In our fly's wiring map (FlyWire, Schlegel et al., Nature 2024) it has 5,177 smell neurons (Kenyon cells), 96 output neurons (MBONs, 35 types) and 331 dopamine neurons: 307 "reward" ones (PAM), 16 "punishment" ones (PPL1) and 8 others (PPL2). It is split into 15 little zones (compartments), each with its own dopamine neurons and output neurons (Aso et al., eLife 2014).
  • Smell neurons: each smell switches on only a small group, about 5–10% of them, like a barcode for that smell (Lin et al., Nat Neurosci 2014; Honegger et al., J Neurosci 2011). In the diagram there are 40 dots and 5 light up per smell (more than in reality, so you can see them). One dot is shared by banana and vinegar.
  • Output neurons vote: some say "go toward", some say "go away". Switch one on artificially and the fly walks toward or away (Aso et al., eLife 2014). On screen, "go toward" is MBON-γ1pedc and "go away" is MBON-γ5β′2a (Perisse et al., Neuron 2016; Owald et al., Neuron 2015).
  • The learning rule: if dopamine arrives while a smell's neurons are active, their connections in that zone get weaker. Only those connections, only in that zone, and only if the smell came first. Scientists measured this directly in the fly brain (Hige et al., Neuron 2015).
  • The twist: learning works by removing a vote. Shock → punishment dopamine (PPL1) weakens the "go toward" vote → the fly avoids the smell. Sugar → reward dopamine (PAM) weakens the "go away" vote → the fly goes toward it.
  • Same chemical, opposite meaning: both use dopamine. What it means depends on which neurons send it and where. Fun fact: insects don't have cortisol. Their version of adrenaline is octopamine, and it also helps with reward: it switches on the reward dopamine neurons (Schwaerzel et al., J Neurosci 2003; Burke et al., Nature 2012; Liu et al., Nature 2012).
  • How scientists test it: the T-maze (Tully & Quinn, 1985). A group of flies smells one odor with mild shocks, then another without. Later they are put at the middle of a T-shaped tube with one smell on each side and they choose. In the lab this becomes a "memory score" (performance index): 0 = no memory, 1 = all flies avoid the shocked smell.
  • Light instead of sugar or shock: with optogenetics scientists switch on the punishment dopamine neurons with light, with no real shock, and the fly still learns to avoid the smell (Claridge-Chang et al., Cell 2009). Switching on the reward neurons works instead of sugar (Liu et al. 2012; Burke et al. 2012). Each dopamine neuron type learns at its own speed and keeps memories for a different time (Aso & Rubin, eLife 2016).
  • Demo: vinegar + shock → Train → Test: most flies (17 of 20) choose banana. Reset. Banana + sugar → Train → Test: most choose banana again, but for the opposite reason. Try "nothing": no dopamine, no change. Train twice and the memory gets stronger. After training vinegar, banana changes a little too, because they share one smell neuron: that is why similar smells get confused.
  • Honest note: this diagram is a toy (each training leaves 45% of the connection). Our whole-brain simulation has fixed wiring: its dopamine neurons are wired in (62,215 connections) but behave like ordinary fast connections, and nothing in it learns yet. A "Learning" tab that adds this rule to the app is being built next.

  • A control experiment, like in a science lab: change one thing and see what happens. Here we scramble the map. Every neuron keeps its outgoing connections (how many, how strong, exciting or inhibiting), but each one now goes to a random neuron. Same digits, same input to the eyes.
  • Whole brain: 91.1% with the real wiring vs 90.1% scrambled. With only 1,000 test digits, that's the same. Central brain 63.6% → 90.8%, descending neurons 62.2% → 87.0% when scrambled.
